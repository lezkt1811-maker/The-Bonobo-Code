# Chapter 8: Evidence and Sources

This chapter gathers the research behind the claims in Chapters 1 through 7. It shows where the evidence is strong, where it is limited, and where the book goes further than the data. Sources marked as still to be verified have not yet been checked against the original papers.

## Bonobos

A 2025 study in *Communications Biology* followed six wild bonobo communities over about thirty years. In those communities, more frequent female coalitions went with greater female power relative to males. Most of the observed coalitions were aimed at males. This is an association, not proof that coalitions cause power, and bonobo females do not always win.

Female coalitions can also be violent. A 2026 report in *Scientific Reports* describes an encounter between groups in which a coalition made up mostly of females was involved in an infant's death. This is why bonobo females should not be described as a peaceful utopia.

A 2014 study in *Nature* compared 18 chimpanzee communities with four bonobo communities and found many more recorded killings among chimpanzees. Lower violence in bonobos is not evidence that female bonds caused it, because ecology and other factors could explain the difference.

A 2019 study in *Hormones and Behavior* (Moscovice et al.) reported that 971 sexual events in one bonobo population were analyzed, and about 65% involved genital rubbing between females. Afterward, female pairs were more likely to stay close, and urinary oxytocin rose more after female-female contact than after heterosexual mating in that small sample. The study shows association and a limited sample. It does not show that the behavior exists for a single purpose or that the results apply to humans.

Across other primates, strong social bonds among females have been linked to survival. In wild baboons, females with stronger bonds had higher infant survival (Silk et al., *Science*, 2003 **[to verify]**).

## Female collective action in human history

Women have built durable collective institutions, and some of them mobilized quickly when they had existing ties.

- **Igbo Women's War (1929, Nigeria).** Women across a region protested against colonial taxation and abuses. Existing women's associations are credited with enabling fast coordination (Van Allen, 1972 **[to verify]**). Other pressures also shaped the outcome.
- **Iceland's women's day off (1975).** Most women stopped paid and unpaid work for a day. Gender-equality politics advanced in the following years. Iceland already had strong unions and women's organizations, so the strike's specific effect is uncertain **[to verify]**.
- **SEWA (Ahmedabad, India, founded 1972).** A union and cooperative of self-employed women that organizes credit and services for informal workers **[to verify outcomes]**.
- **Rotating savings and credit associations.** Women pool money and take turns receiving the pot. These groups are common in West Africa and South Asia and are a long-running form of mutual aid (Ardener, 1964 **[to verify]**).

## Limits the book should keep in view

- Microfinance and group-based lending have shown modest average effects on women's empowerment and income in randomized trials (Banerjee et al., 2015 **[to verify]**). Being in a group does not reliably produce power on its own.
- Female networks can also enforce norms that restrict women, such as policing marriage and reputation.
- Divisions of race, class, and religion have split women's movements at times, and the record is more specific than any single explanation.
- Law, wealth, the control of force, and elite alliances have often mattered more than networks alone.
- Oxytocin studies in humans are small and context-dependent. A measured rise in oxytocin is not proof of a particular feeling or a particular social outcome.

## What this means for the book

The evidence supports the claim that women's relationships with one another can be a source of practical support, information, and collective action. The clearest examples are cases where women already had regular contact through markets, associations, or shared work. The evidence does not show that female bonds always produce power, that bonobo society is a model for humans, or that any one physical practice produces a specific social result.
