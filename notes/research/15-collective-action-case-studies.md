# 15 — Case Studies of Women's Collective Action

The project's chain is *relationship → cooperation → coalition → collective power*. This file tests it on documented cases, including failures. All **[verify]**: facts and figures here come from general knowledge, not checked sources.

## How to read a case

For each case: what network existed beforehand; what it did; what it achieved; and what else could explain the outcome.

## Cases

### 1. Igbo Women's War (Aba, Nigeria, 1929) 🟢

- Women across a region mobilized against British colonial administration (rumors of taxation of women and abuses by warrant chiefs).
- Existing women's institutions and networks (e.g., market, lineage-daughter and village women's associations) are credited with enabling rapid mobilization (Van Allen 1972, "Sitting on a Man").
- Colonial troops killed dozens of protesters; reforms to the warrant-chief system followed.
- **Supports:** pre-existing female networks → fast coordinated action. **Caution:** outcomes were partial and the role of other factors (broader anticolonial pressure) is debated.

### 2. Iceland women's day off (24 Oct 1975) 🟢

- Women stopped paid and unpaid work; the great majority reportedly participated, and a notable gain in gender-equality politics followed in subsequent years (e.g., Vigdís Finnbogadóttir elected president in 1980).
- **Supports:** coordinated action produced visible social leverage. **Caution:** Iceland already had unions, parties and women's organizations; causal weight of the strike alone is uncertain. Use only with sourcing.

### 3. SEWA (India, founded 1972) 🟢

- Union/cooperative of self-employed women (informal workers) formed in Ahmedabad; provides organizing, credit, and social services.
- **Supports:** informal women workers building institutions that did not exist for them. **Caution:** scale and long-run outcomes should be taken from evaluations, not self-reports.

### 4. Rotating savings and credit associations (ROSCAs) 🟢

- Found widely (e.g., West Africa, South Asia, Caribbean); members pool money and take turns receiving the pot; women are a large share of members in many places (Ardener 1964).
- **Supports:** a durable form of female mutual aid with tangible resources. **Caution:** participation is shaped by need and by men's control of other credit.

### 5. Market women's networks (West Africa) 🟢

- Market women's associations have organized trade, credit, dispute resolution and, at times, politics (Clark 1994 on Asante traders).
- **Supports:** economic roles as a base for collective capacity. **Caution:** hierarchy and class divisions exist among traders.

### 6. Self-help groups and microcredit 🔵

- Group-based finance has been associated with some gains in women's participation, but randomized evaluations of microfinance found modest average effects on empowerment and income (Banerjee et al. 2015).
- **Contradictory:** group structure alone does not reliably convert into power.

### 7. Failure/limit cases ⚪

- Some women's organizations were absorbed or controlled by states, parties or churches.
- Divisions of race, class, caste and religion fragmented groups (see `07`).
- Some highly sociable female networks sustained norms that restricted women (e.g., policing marriage and reputation).

## Interpretation 🟡

Where women already had recurring contact through shared economic or social roles (markets, lineages, workplaces), collective action was **faster and cheaper to mobilize**. That is the project's "build relationships before you need them" claim (statement #30) in its most defensible form. It does not show networks are sufficient: legal context, resources, repression and elite alliances determined outcomes.

## Alternative explanations

- Shared grievance or sudden trigger (taxation rumor) may explain mobilization better than networks.
- Selection bias: we remember successful or dramatic cases.

## Open questions

- Build a systematic comparison (successes + failures) rather than cases chosen because they fit.
- Which network feature (density, overlap with economic roles, leadership) mattered most?
