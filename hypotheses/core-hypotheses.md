# Core Hypotheses

Each hypothesis lists: statement, supporting evidence, contradictory evidence, alternative explanations, unanswered questions, and falsification criteria.

Evidence labels: 🟣 fact · 🔵 research finding · 🟢 historical · 🟡 interpretation · 🔴 hypothesis · ⚪ contradictory/limitation.
Items marked **[verify]** are not yet checked against the primary source.

See also: [`supporting-evidence.md`](supporting-evidence.md), [`contradictory-evidence.md`](contradictory-evidence.md), [`open-questions.md`](open-questions.md).

## H1. Female-network fragmentation 🔴

**Statement:** Modern social structures may fragment women's relationships with one another.

**Supporting evidence (candidate):**
- 🔵 US surveys show shrinking core discussion networks and rising reported isolation overall (McPherson, Smith-Lovin & Brashears 2006) **[verify]**. Not sex-specific.
- 🟢 Historical work on nineteenth-century "female worlds" of intimacy and association (Smith-Rosenberg 1975; Scott 1991) shows dense female networks existed in specific settings.
- 🟡 Mid-twentieth-century suburbanization and domestic ideals (Friedan 1963; Coontz 1992) plausibly reduced daytime neighborhood contact for some women.

**Contradictory evidence / limitations:**
- ⚪ Loss of networks is documented for both sexes; not shown to be female-specific.
- ⚪ Some data suggest women maintain more close confidant ties than men on average **[verify]**, which cuts against a female-specific deficit.
- ⚪ Women's labor-force participation and new institutions (schools, workplaces, online) created new contexts for contact.

**Alternative explanations:** general decline in civic participation (Putnam 2000), geographic mobility, work hours, media/technology, urbanization.

**Unanswered:** Has *women-to-women* tie density declined, measured consistently, across which periods and populations?

**Falsification:** Longitudinal network data show no decline (or an increase) in women's ties to other women, or a decline no larger than men's.

## H2. Network-based autonomy 🔴

**Statement:** Women's autonomy may depend partly on access to strong networks of other women.

**Supporting evidence (candidate):**
- 🔵 Social support is associated with better health and survival broadly (Holt-Lunstad et al. 2010) **[verify]**; not specific to female networks.
- 🔵 Some evidence that women's group membership/self-help groups relate to mobility and decision-making in South Asia (e.g., Desai & Joshi 2014) **[verify]**.
- 🟢 Ethnographic accounts of kin/friend networks as survival infrastructure (Stack 1974).

**Contradictory evidence / limitations:**
- ⚪ Reverse causality: more autonomous women may build more networks.
- ⚪ Microfinance/group-based programs show modest or mixed empowerment effects (Banerjee et al. 2015) **[verify]**.
- ⚪ Networks can also enforce norms and restrict women (e.g., mothers-in-law, gossip policing).

**Alternative explanations:** income, education, law, and class explain autonomy; networks may be a correlate.

**Unanswered:** Does network access predict autonomy after controlling for income, education and legal status? Does it need to be *female* ties specifically?

**Falsification:** Female-tie density has no independent association with autonomy measures once economic/legal variables are controlled, or mixed-sex networks work equally well.

## H3. Coalition opportunity 🔴

**Statement:** Repeated female interaction may increase opportunities for cooperation and coalition formation.

**Supporting evidence (candidate):**
- 🔵 Contact and repeated interaction increase liking and cooperation in social psychology generally (mere-exposure, contact, proximity–friendship literature; e.g., Festinger, Schachter & Back 1950) **[verify]**.
- 🔵 Bonobo female coalition frequency associated with female power (Communications Biology 2025) — comparative only. See `research/02`.

**Contradictory evidence / limitations:**
- ⚪ Proximity also breeds conflict and competition, including among women.
- ⚪ Proximity–cooperation findings are not specific to women.

**Alternative explanations:** shared interests or threats, not proximity, may drive coalition; institutions may matter more.

**Unanswered:** Under which conditions does repeated interaction convert into coalition rather than rivalry?

**Falsification:** Natural experiments where women's proximity rises (new workplaces, housing) show no increase in cooperation/coalition relative to controls.

## H4. Social infrastructure 🔴

**Statement:** Female solidarity may depend on actual social infrastructure rather than simply cultural encouragement to "support other women."

**Supporting evidence (candidate):**
- 🔵 Physical places/organizations shape who meets whom (Klinenberg 2018, *Palaces for the People*) **[verify]**.
- 🟢 Women's associations flourished where institutions supported them (Scott 1991; Skocpol 2003).

**Contradictory evidence / limitations:**
- ⚪ Solidarity has emerged with very little formal infrastructure (informal networks, letters, oral networks).
- ⚪ Infrastructure evidence is mostly general, not female-specific.

**Alternative explanations:** motivation, shared threat, and leadership may matter more than places.

**Unanswered:** Which components (places, routines, organizations, online) contribute most?

**Falsification:** Interventions that build spaces/routines for women produce no lasting ties or cooperation beyond cultural messaging alone.

## H5. Re-emergence 🔴

**Statement:** Female networks may repeatedly reappear when conditions allow women to form durable relationships.

**Supporting evidence (candidate):**
- 🟢 Recurring forms: beguinages, convents, mutual-aid societies, clubs, unions, sworn-sisterhoods in parts of southern China, women's sports, online groups (see `research/10`, `research/08`).

**Contradictory evidence / limitations:**
- ⚪ Survivorship/selection bias: we record institutions that lasted or left records.
- ⚪ Male networks also re-emerge; recurrence may reflect general human sociality.
- ⚪ Many women-centered institutions were created by religious or state authorities, not independently.

**Alternative explanations:** institutional sponsorship, economic need, demographic change.

**Unanswered:** Compare re-emergence rates across sexes and societies.

**Falsification:** Systematic evidence that female associations arise no more readily than male ones, or only under top-down sponsorship.

## H6. Collective power 🔴

**Statement:** Dense female networks may increase women's ability to generate collective resources, protection, resilience, and influence.

**Supporting evidence (candidate):**
- 🔵 Community mobilization programs reduced partner violence in a Ugandan cluster-randomized trial (SASA!, Abramsky et al. 2014) **[verify]** — a community-wide intervention, not female-only.
- 🟢 Organizations such as SEWA and women's suffrage networks converted ties into resources and law reform **[verify details]**.
- 🔵 Bonobos: see `research/02` (comparative).

**Contradictory evidence / limitations:**
- ⚪ Dense networks can be closed, parochial, or resource-poor; density does not equal power.
- ⚪ Bonobo results do not license inference to humans.

**Alternative explanations:** law, wealth, military/political control, and organizational resources.

**Unanswered:** How to measure "collective power" independent of the outcome we want to see?

**Falsification:** Communities with denser female networks show no better protection/resilience/influence than matched communities.

## H7. Internal fragmentation 🔴

**Statement:** Women's collective organizations may be weakened when inequalities among women prevent durable coalition.

**Supporting evidence (candidate):**
- 🟢 The 1869 split of US suffrage organizations (NWSA/AWSA) over the Fifteenth Amendment and race (DuBois 1978; Terborg-Penn 1998) **[verify]**.
- 🟢 Critiques of racial and class exclusion in feminist movements (Davis 1981; Crenshaw 1989; Combahee River Collective 1977).

**Contradictory evidence / limitations:**
- ⚪ Movements also fragmented from strategy disputes, funding, repression, and leadership conflict — not only inequality.
- ⚪ Cross-group coalitions succeeded at times (e.g., some labor organizing, later civil-rights-era alliances) **[verify cases]**.

**Alternative explanations:** state repression, internal organizational structure (Freeman 1972), male-dominated opposition.

**Unanswered:** How to separate the effect of inequality among women from other causes of movement decline?

**Falsification:** Comparative case studies show movement durability is unrelated to degree of internal inequality once organizational and repression factors are controlled.
