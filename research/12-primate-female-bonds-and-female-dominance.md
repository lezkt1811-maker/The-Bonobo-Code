# 12 — Female Bonds and Female Dominance Beyond Bonobos

> Bonobos are one comparative case. This file widens the lens: if female bonds matter for power in bonobos, do they matter elsewhere, and is female power ever achieved *without* them? The second question is the stress test.

## Question

Across social mammals, (a) do strong bonds among females improve females' outcomes, and (b) is female social power generally *produced by* female bonds, or can it arise another way?

## Evidence

### A. Female bonds and fitness (supports the general idea)

| Species | Finding | Label | Source **[verify]** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Savannah baboons | Females with stronger, more stable social bonds had higher infant survival | 🔵 | Silk, Alberts & Altmann 2003, *Science* |
| Savannah baboons | Females with strong, consistent social bonds lived longer | 🔵 | Silk et al. 2010, *Current Biology* |
| Female primates generally | Reviews link social integration to survival/reproduction across species | 🔵 | Silk 2007; Cheney & Seyfarth reviews |

These findings concern *bond strength and fitness*, not political power, and not humans.

### B. Female-dominant species with different social logic (stress test)

| Species | Female power? | Basis | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spotted hyenas | Yes, females dominate males; clan hierarchy is matrilineal | Rank inherited through mothers, aggression, and size/androgen factors **[verify]** | Female power with *intense female-female competition*; not primarily a solidarity story |
| Many lemurs (e.g., ring-tailed) | Yes, female dominance over males | Linked to ecology/energetics hypotheses, not coalition-building **[verify]** | Female power without bonobo-style coalitions |
| Elephants | Matriarchal herds led by older females | Kin-based knowledge and cooperation **[verify]** | Female networks are kin-based; males leave |
| Orcas (resident) | Matrilines are core units; post-reproductive females lead | Kin-based **[verify]** | Female social centrality via kin, not unrelated bonds |

### C. Chimpanzees (the closest contrast)

- 🔵 Chimpanzee females generally have weaker, more dispersed bonds than bonobo females and rank below males; this is commonly attributed to ecology (food patchiness) and party size **[verify]**; see Wrangham's work on feeding competition.
- 🔵 This is a clear within-genus contrast and a core reason bonobos are interesting. It also makes *ecology* a plausible confounder for the whole bonobo story.

## Interpretation 🟡

Three different routes to female social power appear in nature:

1. **Coalition route:** unrelated or loosely related females bond and act jointly (bonobos).
2. **Kin/matriline route:** power travels through mothers and daughters (hyenas, elephants, orcas).
3. **Physiological/ecological route:** dominance arises from body size, energetics, or ecology without coalitions (some lemurs).

The project's hypothesis is a version of route 1. The existence of routes 2 and 3 means **female bonds are neither necessary nor sufficient** for female power across mammals; the claim can only be that *where* the coalition route operates, it can matter.

## Alternative explanations

- Ecology (food distribution, predation) can determine both bonding and power.
- Dispersal pattern (which sex leaves) shapes who has kin nearby, and so which bonds are available.

## Contradictory evidence ⚪

- Female-dominant species with weak female bonding exist (route 3).
- Hyena females are dominant yet fiercely competitive with each other; female power ≠ female solidarity.
- Baboon bond-fitness results are association; individuals with better health or higher rank may form better bonds (reverse causality).

## Open questions

- Which route, if any, is the best analogue for humans? (Humans have cooperative breeding, kin ties, *and* unrelated alliances.)
- How much of human female-bond importance is kin-based vs. non-kin?
