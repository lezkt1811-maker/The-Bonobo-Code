# 06 — Patriarchy and Social Power (Mechanisms, Not Slogans)

## Question

What specific mechanisms link social organization to gendered distributions of power, and where do female networks fit among them?

> **Rule:** "Patriarchy" is a label for a family of arrangements. Each use must specify the mechanism being proposed.

## Mechanisms catalog (each is a hypothesis to test)

| Mechanism | How it might reduce women's power | Evidence type to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Property & inheritance law | Limits women's ownership (e.g., coverture in common law) | Legal history 🟢 |
| Residence rules | Separates women from natal kin (patrilocality) | Ethnography |
| Labor division & plough agriculture | Plough use associated with more unequal gender norms (Alesina, Giuliano & Nunn 2013) **[verify]** | Econometrics 🔵 |
| Political exclusion | Suffrage/office restrictions | Legal history 🟢 |
| Violence & threat | Coercion, with limited exit options | Criminology 🔵 |
| Ideology/religion | Norms of female subordination | Comparative religion |
| Household privatization | Reduces collective bargaining | This project's hypothesis 🔴 (`05`) |
| Exclusion from male-dominated networks | Information/patronage denied | Sociology |
| Fragmentation among women | Reduces coalition (H7) | `07` |

Female network strength is proposed as a **moderating/mediating** variable, not a sole cause.

## Defining collective power

A working definition (🟡): the capacity of a group to act together and secure outcomes it could not secure individually. Possible measurable outcomes:

- ability to share resources and mutual aid;
- collective bargaining;
- political organizing and representation;
- protection from violence;
- economic resilience;
- social influence;
- institutional access;
- ability to resist coercion;
- ability to create alternative institutions.

## Evidence

- 🟢 Legal disabilities (coverture; married women's property acts in 19th-century US/UK) shaped women's economic independence.
- 🔵 Cross-cultural status of women varies by subsistence, descent, and residence (Whyte 1978; Sanday 1981) **[verify]**.
- 🔵 Plough hypothesis (Alesina et al. 2013) **[verify]**.
- 🟢 Women's organized networks contributed to suffrage and labor reforms (Hewitt; Scott).

## Interpretation 🟡

Collective autonomy is plausibly partly a **network property**: legal rights need organizations and relationships to be used collectively. This is a hypothesis (H2, H4), not a proven claim.

## Alternative explanations

- Law, wealth, and control of force may dominate; networks may matter mostly when other resources exist.

## Contradictory evidence ⚪

- Societies with strong sex-segregated networks (e.g., some purdah settings) can coexist with low female autonomy.
- Women's legal gains often came with male allies and elite politics, not only female networks.

## Key questions

- *Can individual autonomy exist without collective social infrastructure?*
- *Is collective autonomy partly a network property?*

## Open questions

- How much variation in women's outcomes across societies is explained by network measures once law/economy are controlled?
